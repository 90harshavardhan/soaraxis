Eldercare & caregivers

The people doing the caring need the help first

Eldercare runs on families, home carers and nurses holding an enormous amount in their heads — medications, appointments, small changes in condition, and the paperwork behind all of it. That is where we point our AI work: at the load on the caregiver, not at replacing the care.

Care coordination

Scattered updates from family, home carers and clinicians turned into one shared picture — so the next person on shift starts informed instead of guessing.

Less admin, more presence

Medication schedules, appointment prep, insurance forms and visit notes drafted in the background — giving hours back to the person who should be in the room, not at a desk.

Early, gentle signals

Quiet drift in sleep, mobility, appetite or routine surfaced as a prompt for a human to check in. A nudge to a carer — never an autonomous clinical decision.

Answers at 2am

A plain-language companion for the questions that arrive out of hours — what this medication interacts with, what a symptom might mean, when it warrants a call. Always pointed back to a clinician for the decision.

How we hold the line

Older adults are not a low-stakes test bed. A confident wrong answer about a medication is not a bug report — it is harm. So we build this work under tighter constraints than anything else we do, and we would rather ship something narrow that we can stand behind.

  • Assistive, not autonomous. AI drafts, summarises and flags. A human decides anything clinical.
  • The older adult is a participant. Not a monitored object — consent and visibility by default.
  • Dignity over surveillance. The least data that does the job, and a clear reason for every bit of it.
  • Escalation is a feature. Knowing when to stop and fetch a person is designed in, not bolted on.
